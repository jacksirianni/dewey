## 0. Two decisions this document makes that the memo left open

Both are load-bearing. If you disagree, change them before you print anything.

**Decision A — the reader profiles are anonymous during the legibility task.** §8.2 requires a named person and a photo in condition **P**. §6 requires the profile cards to carry "no prose, no bio." Those are the same requirement at two different moments, not a contradiction. So:

- **Phase 1 (legibility + choice):** four profile cards labelled **Reader A / B / C / D**. No name. No face. No occupation. Library grid, verdict marks, overlap strip. Nothing else. This is the only way the clause "from library + rating overlap **alone**" is literally true, and it suppresses source recognition at the exact moment recognition would corrupt the judgment.
- **Phase 2 (P / N / G):** the reader the participant chose is **revealed** — name, greyscale photo, byline — and becomes the named person on card P.

This also means **P's person is always a reader the participant selected themselves.** That is the strongest fair test of attribution available on paper, and it is the version of P that Dewey would actually ship.

**Decision B — the reader library grid is reusable; only the overlap strip is per-participant.** The 24-cover grid is selected by a mechanical rule that never looks at the participant. Four laminated A4 cards serve all twenty sessions. The participant-specific arithmetic arrives as a 190 × 55 mm gummed strip plus a handful of blue dot stickers. This is what takes founder prep to ~25 min/participant.

# HALF ONE — Sourcing the four real readers

## 1. The arithmetic that governs source choice

This is the single constraint that eliminates most obvious candidates, and it has to be stated before the source list.

A participant's intake library is **20–25 titles**. To land R1–R3 at "engineered density" (8–12 shared books) the reader's transcribed list must be **long**. A ten-book year-end list cannot overlap a 25-book library by eight titles; it cannot overlap it by three. The expected overlap between a 25-title library and a 10-title list, even among readers of the same kind, is well under one.

> **Eligibility floor: a candidate list is unusable below 60 transcribed titles. Target 100–120.**

This does not mean abandoning published lists. It means **cumulating a named person's recurring published record** rather than lifting one instalment of it. A critic who has published a books-of-the-year list for eight years has published ~80 titles under a byline. A bookseller with a staff page at Powell's has ~40–70 signed shelf-talkers accumulated over years. A columnist who logs every book they read has hundreds.

**Cumulation is transcription, not authoring.** The memo's "zero authored titles" survives intact. What you are doing is copying a person's public record into a spreadsheet.

---

## 2. The five eligible source types

For each: where it actually lives, what you transcribe, and the eligibility notes.

### Type 1 — The recurring reading-log column

**Where it lives.** A named writer who logs, in print, every book they read on a fixed cadence. The canonical instance is **Nick Hornby's "Stuff I've Been Reading"** in *The Believer* — every column opens with "Books Bought" and "Books Read" and then discusses them. **The Millions' "A Year in Reading"** is the same shape distributed across many contributors: each essay is one named person's account of their year's reading, typically 15–40 titles, and the series has run annually for well over a decade, so a repeat contributor accumulates a long record under one byline. **The Paris Review's "Staff Picks"** is a weekly column with recurring named contributors.

**What you transcribe.** Title, author, and the stated verdict, for every book named in every instalment, most recent first, up to 120. Ignore books mentioned only as context ("as Nabokov once wrote") — transcribe only books the person states they read.

**Notes.** Best source type available. Long, per-title verdicts in the person's own words, unambiguously public, byline photo usually present on the contributor page. This is your most likely R1.

### Type 2 — The signed independent-bookseller staff page

**Where it lives.** Shops that publish **per-staffer** pages, not anonymous shop-wide picks. Real, longstanding examples: **Powell's Books** (Portland) publishes staff picks attributed to individual booksellers; **Greenlight Bookstore** (Brooklyn), **Books Are Magic** (Brooklyn), **McNally Jackson** (NYC), **Elliott Bay Book Company** (Seattle), **Green Apple Books** (San Francisco), **City Lights** (San Francisco), **Skylight Books** (Los Angeles), **Politics and Prose** (DC), **Left Bank Books** (St. Louis), **Semicolon** (Chicago), and in the UK **Daunt Books**, **Blackwell's**, **Shakespeare and Company** (Paris), **Topping & Company**. Shelf-talkers are signed with the bookseller's first name; the web staff-picks pages usually carry a full name and a photo.

**What you transcribe.** Every book on that named bookseller's page, plus their shelf-talker text reduced to a verdict level. If the shop archives past picks, cumulate backwards.

**Notes.** These are *endorsements by construction* — every entry is a recommendation, so verdict level is uniformly high, which weakens verdict as a discriminator. Compensate by relying on popularity tier for evidence ranking. Length is the usual failure: check the count before you fall in love with the person.

### Type 3 — The critic's cumulative year-end record

**Where it lives.** A named critic's "best books of the year" lists, stacked across every year the outlet has published them. Real recurring formats: the **New York Times** critics' individual top-ten lists; the **Guardian**'s "Best books of the year" (which runs both critic picks and the authors-choose-their-favourites round-up); the **Financial Times** books of the year; the **TLS** and **New Statesman** contributors' books of the year; **Vulture**, **The Atlantic**, **Slate**, **The White Review**, **4Columns**. Also a critic's own outlet **author/contributor page**, which lists every book they reviewed — a much longer record, with the verdict recoverable from each review.

**What you transcribe.** Year, title, author, and where it sat on the list (a ranked #1 is a stronger stated verdict than an unranked mention).

**Notes.** Skews hard to the recent frontlist and to a single publishing era. That is a taste axis, not a defect — this is your "contemporary-only, prize-adjacent" pole. Overlap with participants who read backlist will be near zero, which makes a critic list a strong **R4 candidate**.

### Type 4 — The published "my favourite books" questionnaire

**Where it lives.** The **Guardian's books questionnaire** ("The book I wish I'd written…", "The book that changed me…"), the **New York Times Book Review's "By the Book"** weekly Q&A, the **Financial Times**' and **Times**' equivalents, and **Five Books** (fivebooks.com), where a named expert selects and *ranks* five books on a subject and explains each at length. Literary podcasts with a recurring list segment — **Backlisted** (each episode is one book plus the hosts' and guest's side-reading), **Literary Friction**, **Between the Covers** — produce a transcribable per-episode record.

**What you transcribe.** Every title named, plus the question it answered — the question *is* the verdict ("the book I reread most" outranks "the book I'm currently reading").

**Notes.** A single instalment is 10–25 titles — **below the floor on its own**. Usable only when the person has multiple instalments (several Five Books interviews, a By the Book plus a year-end list plus a podcast appearance) that you cumulate under one byline. Richest verdict data of any source type.

### Type 5 — The self-published public shelf

**Where it lives.** A **StoryGraph**, **Goodreads**, or **LibraryThing** profile that a publicly-bylined person has themselves linked from a professional bio or masthead page. Also personal-site "what I read this year" pages and Substack reading logs with per-book star ratings.

**What you transcribe.** Title, author, star rating, date read.

**Notes.** The only source class that gives you **hundreds of titles with a numeric rating attached** — mechanically the cleanest. It is also the class with the weakest publication norm, so it carries a hard gate:

> **Gate: use a public shelf only if the person has a public byline in a publishing venue AND has themselves linked that shelf from a public professional page.** A shelf that is merely technically visible is not a published list. If you have to search for it, it does not qualify.

## 3. The verdict scale

Real lists do not have five stars. Rather than invent a rating, **transcribe the verdict the source states and map it onto a fixed three-level scale.** The legend prints on every profile card, so the participant knows exactly what they are looking at.

| Mark | Means | Mapped from |
|---|---|---|
| **● ● ●** | **Singled out.** They named this as one of their best, ranked it, reread it, or gave it 5 stars. | Year-end list entry; Five Books selection; "the book I reread most"; ★★★★★; a shelf-talker that says "my favourite book in the shop" |
| **● ●** | **Recommended.** They put it in front of other people in print. | A staff pick; a positive review; ★★★★; "loved it" in a reading-log column |
| **●** | **Read, no stated verdict.** It is on the record; they said nothing about it. | "Books Read" with no comment; ★★★ or below; a bare shelf entry |

**Rules.**
1. The mark is a transcription, never a judgment. If the source is ambiguous, it is **●**.
2. Column `SourceVerdictRaw` in the spreadsheet holds the person's actual words. You must be able to defend every mark by pointing at that column.
3. A reader whose source cannot produce **at least 15 titles at ● ● ●** is ineligible for R1–R3 (it cannot supply the condition-card candidate pool). It remains eligible for R4.

## 4. Eligibility screen

Run this on every candidate before it enters the pool. Five checks, all binary.

| # | Check | Fail action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **≥60 titles** after cumulation (target 100–120) | Apply the cumulation ladder (§7). If still short, retire. |
| 2 | **Per-title verdicts recoverable** for ≥60% of titles | Retire from R1–R3; keep as R4 candidate. |
| 3 | **≥15 titles at ● ● ●** | Retire from R1–R3; keep as R4 candidate. |
| 4 | **A published byline photo exists** at the source or on the outlet's contributor/staff page | Retire entirely. Condition P requires a face; a reader who cannot serve as P is not usable. |
| 5 | **The list is editorially published or self-linked from a public bio** (§2 Type 5 gate) | Retire entirely. No exceptions. |

## 5. The selection axes

The four readers must differ on **how they read**, not on what genre they read. Genre difference is the trivial version of this test and it makes legibility too easy — anyone can tell a crime shelf from a poetry shelf. The question is whether a participant can read *a mind* off a shelf.

Score every candidate on all five axes. Record the scores in the `Readers` sheet before selection.

| Axis | Pole − | Pole + |
|---|---|---|
| **A · Era of taste** | Backlist / canon / out-of-print — reads mostly books older than they are | Frontlist-only — reads this season |
| **B · Relationship to popularity** | Small-press, translated, obscure; visibly avoids the obvious | Comfortable with bestsellers and prize lists; reads what everyone reads |
| **C · What they want from a book** | Sentence-level pleasure, formal risk, difficulty | Argument, information, plot, transport |
| **D · Reading pattern** | Completist / rereader — goes deep on few authors | Promiscuous — one book each from fifty writers |
| **E · Breadth of form** | Narrow — one form dominates (novels only, essays only) | Omnivorous — fiction, criticism, poetry, memoir, science |

**Requirement:** across the four chosen readers, **each axis must be spanned** — at least one reader scoring − and one scoring + on every axis. Two readers may not sit within one point of each other on more than two axes.

**The poor-match requirement.** At least one of R1–R3 must be a reader **most participants will not want to follow** — not because the overlap is thin, but because the taste is genuinely uncongenial in kind (the doctrinaire canon-only reader; the reader of exclusively very long very difficult books; the reader of exclusively very commercial ones). This is not decoration.

> If every participant likes every reader, the choice task has measured agreeableness, not legibility. A live "no, not for me" is what makes the articulation falsifiable.

This is a **separate requirement from R4.** R4 tests *density*. The poor-match reader tests *discrimination*. Conflating them costs you one of the two findings.

## 6. The exact selection procedure

Follow it in order. Do not reorder steps 5 and 6 — the sealed draw must precede the overlap table or R4 is contaminated.

**Rule of twelve.**

> **Gather 12 candidate lists before you recruit anyone.** Expect the eligibility screen to kill 3–4. You need 8 survivors: 3 become R1–R3, 1 becomes R4, and 4 are spares for the failure modes in §7. Below 8 survivors, gather more before proceeding — do not run the study on a thin pool, because a thin pool forces you to pick R1–R3 on availability rather than on axis coverage, and axis coverage is what the legibility test is made of.

| Step | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Gather 12 candidates, ≥2 from each of the five source types, deliberately spanning both poles of every axis. Record source URL, format, date range, and byline-photo URL. | `Readers` sheet, 12 rows |
| **2** | Run the §4 eligibility screen. Retire failures with a reason recorded. | 8+ eligible |
| **3** | Transcribe each survivor to 100–120 titles (§8 sheet spec). Assign popularity tier to every title (§13). | `R#_List` sheets |
| **4** | Score all survivors on axes A–E. | Axis matrix |
| **5** | **Seal R4 now.** Number the survivors. Draw one at random — `=INDEX(A:A, RANDBETWEEN(1,n))`, run **once**, screenshot the result, paste it into a doc, date it, and **do not open it again until step 7.** Draw a second, different candidate as **Envelope B**, seal it the same way. **You have not yet computed a single overlap number.** | R4 and R4-alt, sealed |
| **6** | Run **calibration intakes** with the first 6 recruited participants. Compute mean shared-book count for every remaining survivor against those 6 libraries (§8 worksheet). Select R1–R3 as: the highest-mean candidate; then the highest-mean candidate that spans an unspanned axis pole; then the highest-mean candidate satisfying the **poor-match requirement**. If the axis-span requirement cannot be met from the top five by mean, take a lower-mean candidate — **axis coverage outranks density.** | R1, R2, R3 fixed |
| **7** | Open the R4 envelope. Whatever it says is R4. Record its calibration mean **after** the fact, for the readout only. Never for selection. | R4 fixed |
| **8** | If R4 duplicates an R1–R3 selection, use Envelope B. This is the only permitted substitution. | Four readers |
| **9** | Print the four profile cards (§10). Cards are now frozen for all 20 sessions. | Materials |

**The pre-registration line.** Before step 6, write in the study log, dated:

> "R1–R3 will be selected on calibration overlap subject to axis coverage and the poor-match requirement. R4 was drawn at random on {date} before any overlap was computed and will be used regardless of its density. Whatever R4's overlap turns out to be is the finding, not a defect."

**Early-abort check at step 6.** If **no** candidate reaches a mean of 5 shared books across the 6 calibration participants, stop and read decision-tree row 4. The engineered-density premise has failed before the study began, and what you should run instead is the density study.

## 7. Failure modes and their repairs

**The list is too short (<60 after the first pass).** Apply the **cumulation ladder**, in order, stopping when you clear 60:

1. Extend backwards through the same recurring format — more years of the year-end list, more instalments of the column, more episodes of the segment.
2. Add the same person's other published book-list appearances under the same byline: a By the Book, a Five Books interview, a "books that made me" questionnaire, a podcast list segment.
3. Add their outlet contributor page — every book they have reviewed, with the verdict taken from the review.
4. Add their self-linked public shelf, **only if the §2 Type 5 gate passes**.

Cap the reach-back at **five years**. Beyond that you are transcribing a different person. If you are still under 60 titles at the end of the ladder, **retire the candidate.**

**The list is too obscure to overlap with anyone.** This is not a failure; it is a result arriving early. The mechanical rule in step 6 already excludes it from R1–R3 (low calibration mean). Keep it in the R4 draw pool — a reader whose taste is real and specific and shares almost nothing with a given participant is precisely the cold-start condition Dewey has to survive.

**R4 lands at zero shared books for a given participant.** Print the strip honestly: `0 shared books.` A zero-overlap reader is a legitimate stimulus and "can you read a stranger with nothing in common?" is a question worth the card. Use Envelope B only if R4 hits zero for **more than half** the participants — and record that substitution prominently in the readout, because a pool that produces zero at that rate is itself the finding.

**One reader dominates every participant's choice.** Expected and fine. Report the count. Do not swap the reader mid-study; a change of stimulus midway destroys comparability across the 20.

**The prohibited repair.**

> **Never add, remove, or reorder titles on a reader's list to improve overlap.** The moment you touch a shelf, you have re-created the founder's-taste confound that §7.3 of the memo exists to eliminate, and you will not be able to tell from the transcript that you did. If a list will not work, retire the whole list. Retiring is free; tuning is fatal.

## 8. Attribution lines — verbatim

Three lines, used at three moments. Print them exactly.

### 8.1 Phase 1 — on every profile card, footer, 7pt

```
This shelf belongs to a real person. It is transcribed from a book list
they published publicly. Who they are is on the back of this card, and
we will go through it with you at the end of the session.
```

The name genuinely is on the back — sealed with a removable label. The participant may look at any time; if they do, record it and follow §9's recognition rule.

### 8.3 Debrief — read aloud, verbatim, end of session

```
"Before you go — three things about what you just looked at.

All four shelves belonged to real people. Nothing was made up. The books,
the ratings, the marks — all of it came from lists those people published
publicly, under their own names, and I've written the sources on this
sheet so you can look them up yourself.

None of them know about this. They haven't endorsed Dewey, they haven't
recommended anything to you specifically, and I want to be sure you leave
with that straight — the recommendation card said a real person rated a
real book highly, which is true, and it did not say they recommended it
to you, which would not be.

The overlap numbers were real too. I computed those from the books you
told me about on the intake call, before today.

Is there anything on any of these cards you'd want me to take out of the
study?"
```

### 8.4 Legal and ethical posture — the six commitments

1. **Public only.** Every title comes from an editorially published list, or a public shelf the person has themselves linked from a public professional page. Nothing scraped from behind a login. Nothing inferred.
2. **Attributed and linked.** Name, byline, venue, date range, URL — shown at reveal, printed at debrief, retained in the log.
3. **No implied endorsement.** No card says "recommends," "suggests," "thinks you'd like," or "for you." The permitted verbs are **rated**, **named**, **listed**, **read**, **picked**. The debrief states the distinction out loud.
4. **No fabricated persona.** The person's real public byline, or nothing. Never a first-name-only invented character sitting on a real shelf.
5. **Session-bounded materials.** Photos are printed greyscale at 24 × 32 mm, used in the room, and collected at the end. No participant photographs the cards; no image or real name appears in the readout, in any deck, or in any external document — the readout says "R2," full stop.
6. **Recognition = exclusion.** §9.

If any of this makes you uneasy, the memo's fallback stands (unresolved item 2): four invented readers, ~2 days of authoring, and you accept the confound in writing. Decide before you transcribe, not after.

## 9. The recognition check

Ask it once per reader at reveal, verbatim:

```
"Have you come across this person or this list before — do you read them,
follow them, know them, anything?"
```

Record on the session log: `RECOGNIZED / SUSPECTED / NO`.

**If RECOGNIZED or SUSPECTED:** that reader's data is excluded for that participant — the legibility judgment, the choice, and, if that was the chosen reader, the whole P/N/G block for that session. Say so plainly: *"That's genuinely useful, thank you — I'll set that one aside."* Do not re-run with a substitute reader; a mid-session substitution is worse than a missing cell. Note it and move on. Log the excluded cells; report the count in the readout.

### 10.1 Field spec

| Field | Value | Spec |
|---|---|---|
| Letter | A / B / C / D | 18pt caps. **Mapping letter→reader rotates per participant** (`(participant_index + reader_index) mod 4`) so letter position never correlates with reader. |
| Cover grid | 24 covers, 3 rows × 8 | 34 × 52 mm each, 6 mm gutters. Sourced from Open Library covers API at the ISBN in the sheet. |
| Grid selection | **Mechanical, participant-blind** | ● ● ● titles, most recent first, up to 16 → then ● ●, most recent first, up to 6 → then ●, up to 2. Fill any shortfall from the next level down. Never hand-picked. |
| Verdict mark | ● ● ● / ● ● / ● | 8pt, centred under each cover |
| Legend | Fixed, all four cards | 8pt |
| Count line | `24 of {N} books shown.` | 8pt italic. Honest about the truncation. |
| Overlap strip zone | 190 × 55 mm blank, ruled | Receives the gummed per-participant strip |
| Footer | §8.1 verbatim | 7pt |
| Back | Sealed reveal label | Removable sticker over the §8.2 block |

### 10.2 What must NOT appear on this card

Absolute. If any of these is present, the card is testing something else.

- No name, pseudonym, handle, initials, or byline.
- No photograph, avatar, or illustration of a person.
- No occupation, city, age, gender, or nationality.
- No bio, blurb, tagline, or "about."
- No prose of any kind about the reader — no "reads widely in translation," no "prefers short novels."
- No review text, no quoted shelf-talker, no pull-quote, no annotation from the source.
- No genre labels, tags, or category headers on the grid.
- No aggregate statistics — no average rating, no books-per-year, no "mostly fiction."
- No source venue, publication name, or URL on the front.
- No Dewey branding, logo, or product framing.

**What is permitted:** covers, titles and authors as printed on those covers, verdict marks, the legend, the count line, the overlap strip, the §8.1 footer. Nothing else.

### 11.5 Condition G — impersonal framing line only, no named books

**Rail:** flat 10% warm-grey tint block, 24 × 32 mm, containing the word **SELECTED** set 8pt caps, letterspaced, rotated 90°, ranged bottom. This is the bookshop shelf-card idiom — a printed tab, not an absence.

**Copy pattern:** the framing line typeset across lines 1–3, **vertically centred in the 5-line body zone** (one blank line above, one below), at the same 9pt/12pt as P and N.

```
line 1:  (blank — optical centring)
line 2:  {FRAMING CLAUSE 1}
line 3:  {FRAMING CLAUSE 2}
line 4:  {FRAMING CLAUSE 3}
line 5:  (blank — optical centring)
```

**Slot rules.** Three clauses, 90–120 characters total. **Every clause must be a checkable fact about this specific book**, and you record the citation for each in the `G_Truth` column of the session log before printing. **No named books. No person. No rating. No "for you," no "readers like you," no "trending," no count of any kind.**

**How G is padded to equal height without looking padded.** Four devices, all structural rather than verbal — you are never adding filler words:

1. **The stock does the work.** All three cards are 148 mm. Vertical extent is matched by the object, not by the copy.
2. **The rail is occupied.** G's tint block with the rotated SELECTED tab fills the same 24 × 32 mm as P's photo and N's cover strip, so the left column never reads as empty.
3. **Symmetric optical centring.** The framing line sits with equal air above and below inside the ruled body zone. Whitespace distributed evenly reads as composition; whitespace pushed to the bottom reads as a hole. This is the difference between the two, and it is entirely a matter of where the blank lines go.
4. **The edition line and hairline are present.** The card is closed at the bottom on all three, so nothing trails off.

Then run the squint test. If G still reads as the odd one out, the fix is to lengthen the framing clauses to fill line 4 — **never** to enlarge the type or the cover.

### 13.4 Popularity tier — the one hand judgment that remains

Four tiers, assigned once per title, by a rule blunt enough to be repeatable:

| Tier | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Household name | Your non-reading relative has heard of it |
| **2** | Widely read literary | Front table at a chain bookshop; a major prize winner or shortlistee |
| **3** | Known within a readership | A serious reader of that kind knows it; not stocked everywhere |
| **4** | Small press / backlist / out of print / untranslated-until-recently | You had to look it up |

Assign tier from **Open Library edition count** as the tie-breaker when you are unsure: ≥40 editions → 1; 15–39 → 2; 4–14 → 3; ≤3 → 4. Use the count when you hesitate; overrule it only with a note in an adjacent comment.

### 13.5 The normalization key

Drop into `C2` on every list sheet and fill down. Strips articles, punctuation, case, and matches on title + author surname.

```
=IF(A2="","",
 LET(
   t,  LOWER(TRIM(A2)),
   t1, REGEXREPLACE(t, "^(the |a |an )", ""),
   t2, REGEXREPLACE(t1, "[^a-z0-9]", ""),
   parts, SPLIT(TRIM(B2), " "),
   sur, REGEXREPLACE(LOWER(INDEX(parts,1,COLUMNS(parts))), "[^a-z]", ""),
   t2 & "|" & sur))
```

**Manual reconciliation pass — 5 minutes, mandatory.** Translated titles, reissues under a new title, and hyphenated surnames will miss. After filling down, sort both key columns and eyeball for near-misses. Fix by overwriting the key on the *reader* sheet, never the participant sheet, and log the fix. Expect 2–4 per reader.

### 13.6 `Overlap` — the working sheet

| Col | Header | Formula |

| K | `SurnameKey` | `=IF(B2="","",LOWER(INDEX(SPLIT(TRIM(B2)," "),1,COLUMNS(SPLIT(TRIM(B2)," ")))))` |

**Headline count** (goes on the strip):

```
=SUM(F2:F30)
```

**The three evidence titles — the mechanical selector.** One formula. It sorts by evidence score descending, then distinctiveness descending, then author surname ascending, and takes the top three.

```
=ARRAY_CONSTRAIN(
   SORT(
     FILTER({A2:A30, K2:K30, L2:L30, J2:J30, H2:H30}, F2:F30=1),
     4, FALSE,
     5, FALSE,
     2, TRUE),
   3, 3)
```

Columns returned: `Title`, `SurnameKey`, `AgreementLine` — drop straight into the P and N card slots, in the order returned, no reordering.

**The rule, stated in words so it cannot be quietly broken:**

> **Evidence score E = D × A**, where **D** is the distinctiveness weight from popularity tier (Tier 1 = 1, Tier 2 = 2, Tier 3 = 4, Tier 4 = 7) and **A** is agreement (the reader's verdict level 3/2/1 if the participant rated it 4 or 5; otherwise 0). Rank all shared books by E descending, break ties by D descending, then by author surname ascending. **The top three are the evidence titles.** They go on cards P and N in that order.
>
> **You do not choose them. You do not reorder them. You do not skip one because you think another is more persuasive.** If the top three include a book that feels like a weak card, that is the finding arriving on schedule — record it in the log and print it anyway. The moment you hand-pick the evidence, the treatment becomes your judgment and P vs N measures nothing.

**Under-evidenced pairs.** If fewer than three shared books have E > 0, the sort still returns three rows (E = 0 rows sort last, ordered by D then surname), and their `AgreementLine` correctly reads `Neither of you said more.` Every statement stays true. Record `EvidenceCount = COUNTIF(J2:J30,">0")` in the log. If `EvidenceCount = 0` for the chosen reader, note it prominently — that session's P/N contrast is running on bare co-readership.

### 13.7 The three candidate books — also mechanical

Run against the **chosen** reader after Phase 1.

```
Step 1  Participant's modal tier:
        =MODE(P01_Lib!E2:E30)

Step 2  Eligible pool — the reader's top-verdict books the participant
        has not read and is not already planning to read:
        =FILTER({R2_List!A:A, R2_List!B:B, R2_List!G:G, R2_List!F:F},
                R2_List!E:E = 3,
                COUNTIF(P01_Lib!$C:$C, R2_List!C:C) = 0,
                R2_List!F:F = <modal tier>)

Step 3  Sort by SourceDate descending, then author surname ascending.
        Take the top 3.

Step 4  If fewer than 3 in the modal tier, widen to modal tier ± 1,
        taking the LOWER tier first (more obscure before more popular),
        and re-sort. Record the widening in the log.

Step 5  Assign to P / N / G by the Latin square (§11.6).
```

**Verify unread out loud** before laying the cards down: *"Have you read any of these three? Started any of them? Own any of them?"* Any yes → swap that one card for row 4 of the Step 3 sort, and record the swap. Do not proceed with a book they have read.

### 13.8 `Log` columns

`ParticipantID` · `IntakeDate` · `SessionDate` · `IncumbentTitle` · `IncumbentQuote` · `LetterMapping` · `SharedCount_R1..R4` · `ChosenReader` · `ChoiceReasonVerbatim` · `SpecificFalsifiable (Y/N)` · `Recognition_R1..R4` · `EvidenceCount` · `EvidenceTitles` · `TierWidened (Y/N)` · `CandidateSwaps` · `P_Book` · `N_Book` · `G_Book` · `G_TruthCitations` · `PhysicalOrder` · `SlotOutcome (INCUMBENT / P / N / G)` · `WhyVerbatim` · `SquintTestPass` · `Notes`

**`SpecificFalsifiable` is the stop-condition column.** Code it against a written rubric, blind to condition, in a single pass after all 20 sessions — never in the room. A description counts only if it could be **wrong**: "she likes short strange novels by women, mostly European, and she doesn't finish long books" is specific and falsifiable. "She's a serious reader" and "good taste" are not. Count them. `≤8 of 20` and you stop.

**Reporting rule.** Counts out of 20. **"14 of 20," never "70%," never "68.3%," never a p-value.** State it in the readout in these words: *This study cannot produce statistical significance and is not attempting to. A 14/20 split is inside the range twenty coin flips produce. The counts are reported without a verdict.*

## 15. The five ways this instrument can be quietly broken

Pin this above the printer.

1. **Hand-picking the evidence titles.** The most tempting failure and the most fatal. The sort in §13.6 returns three rows; those are the three. Print them even when they look weak.
2. **N not carrying P's exact evidence set.** If N's three titles differ from P's in content or order, you are no longer measuring attribution, you are measuring two different personalizations. Check this against the sheet before every print, not from memory.
3. **Adding a title to a reader's list.** Retire the list instead. Always.
4. **Letting the participant see a name before the choice.** The reveal label stays sealed through Phase 1. If it comes off early, log it and treat that reader as RECOGNIZED.
5. **Softening the null.** "Leaving your own book is completely normal" is read once, flat, in the words in §12.2. Warmth here reads as permission to please you; apology here reads as a wrong answer. Say it and stop talking.
