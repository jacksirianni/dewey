## 0. What these two instruments are for, in one paragraph

The intake call does four jobs and no others: it confirms the participant is the wedge reader; it extracts enough of their real reading history to compute a real overlap against four real published lists; it captures **their own stated next book** before they have seen anything from Dewey; and it captures **one real friend** they would genuinely send a book to. The commitment-displacement instrument then makes that stated next book cost something. A Dewey card only counts if the participant physically removes their own book from the only slot on the table. The output that matters is not the choice. **The output that matters is the sentence they say immediately after.**

## PART ONE — THE INTAKE

## 1. Two-stage structure

Do not run screeners on a live call. The call is twenty minutes and every minute of it must be spent on things that require a human.

| Stage | Tool | Duration | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage A** | Google Form, sent at recruitment | 3 min, self-serve | Consent, screener, logistics, follow-up channel |
| **Stage B** | Video or phone call, moderator-led, recorded | **20 min** | Reading history, ratings, incumbent book, friend |

A participant who has not completed Stage A does not get a Stage B slot. No exceptions — a screener administered live wastes six minutes and biases the answers, because people who are already on a call with a founder tell the founder what he wants.

### 1.1 The cover story (true, incomplete, disclosed at debrief)

Use this exact wording everywhere — recruitment post, Form intro, call opening, calendar invite:

> "I'm running a small study about how people decide what to read next. There are two parts: a twenty-minute call about your reading, and a forty-five-minute session about a week later where I'll show you some things on paper and ask what you think. No app, no software, nothing to install."

This is true. It omits the hypothesis, which is correct — a participant told "we're testing whether attribution beats personalization" produces useless data. The full purpose, the identity of the four readers and their sources, and the role of their stated next book are disclosed at the debrief in §16.

### 1.2 Recruitment note that must go in the confirmation email

> "One small favour: take the call somewhere near your books, or with your Kindle / Libby / StoryGraph / Goodreads open on another screen. There's a bit where I'll ask you to just read titles off a shelf, and it goes much faster if you can actually see one."

This single line is worth more than any other change to the protocol. Prompted recall from a visible shelf roughly doubles title yield against free recall in a fixed time budget. Do not skip it.

## 2. Stage A — the Google Form

**Form title:** Reading study — a few quick questions
**Description field:** the cover story from §1.1, verbatim, plus: "Takes about three minutes. If you're a fit I'll send you a link to book a twenty-minute call."

### Section 1 — Consent and eligibility

**Q1.** Are you 18 or over?
`Yes` / `No` *(No → end of form, thanks page)*

**Q2.** This study has two parts: one 20-minute call and one 45-minute session about a week later, plus two short text or email messages after that. Can you commit to all of it?
`Yes` / `No` / `Not sure — tell me more`

**Q3.** Both parts will be audio-recorded so I don't have to take notes while we talk. Recordings are kept private, are not published, and are deleted at the end of the study. Transcripts are stored under a code, not your name. Is that OK?
`Yes` / `No` *(No → end of form)*

**Q4.** After the session I'd like to send you two short messages — one about a week later, one about three weeks later. Which is easiest for you?
`Text` / `WhatsApp` / `Email` / `I'd rather not` *(the last option is allowed and recorded; it does not disqualify, it just means no follow-up row)*

**Q5.** Best number or address for that: `[short answer]`

### Section 2 — Screener

**Q6.** Roughly how many books did you finish in the last twelve months? Best guess is fine — most people underestimate, so if you're between two numbers, take the higher one.
`0–4` / `5–9` / `10–19` / `20–30` / `31–50` / `More than 50`

> **Screen:** `10–19` and `20–30` are the target. Accept `5–9` and `31–50` up to a combined maximum of 4 of the 20 participants, and record them. Reject `0–4` and `More than 50`. The wedge is 10–30/year; a 60-book-a-year reader is a different animal and will dominate the overlap arithmetic.

**Q7.** In the last three months, have you recommended a specific book to a specific person?
`Yes` / `No` / `Can't remember`

> **Screen:** `No` is a reject. This is the behavioural proxy for "books are part of their identity" and it is far more reliable than asking whether books are part of their identity, which everyone says yes to.

**Q8.** Do you work, or have you worked in the last five years, in publishing, bookselling, literary criticism, literary agenting, or as a librarian?
`Yes` / `No`

> **Screen:** `Yes` is a reject. Two reasons, both real: they read differently from the wedge, and they are much more likely to recognise the sources behind the four readers, which forces exclusions under §7 of the memo.

**Q9.** Do you read year-end "best books of the year" lists, follow any book critics, or listen to book podcasts?
`Regularly` / `Sometimes` / `Rarely or never`

> **Not a screen.** This is the **recognition-risk covariate**. Record it. `Regularly` participants are the ones most likely to say "wait, is this the Guardian one?" — which is a valid finding, not a spoiled session, but it must be predictable in advance so the moderator is not surprised. Cap `Regularly` at 6 of 20 so the sample is not tilted toward list-literate readers.

**Q10.** Do you use any app or site to track your reading?
`Goodreads` / `StoryGraph` / `Fable` / `Libby or a library app` / `A spreadsheet or notes app` / `Nothing` / `Other: ___` *(multi-select)*

> Not a screen. Records whether the shelf sweep in §4 will be fast or slow.

**Q11.** Do you read mostly in English?
`Yes` / `No — mostly ___`

> **Screen:** `No` is a reject for this study only. All four reader lists are English-language published lists; overlap against a Spanish-language reading history is not computable from them and the participant deserves better than a wasted hour.

**Q12.** Have you heard of, seen, or used an app called Dewey?
`Yes` / `No`

> **Screen:** `Yes` is a reject. Also reject anyone personally known to the founder — do not ask this on the form, check it against the roster by hand.

**Q13.** How did you hear about this study? `[short answer]`

### Form logic

Auto-reject responses route to a neutral thanks page: *"Thanks — you're not a fit for this particular study, but I appreciate you taking the time."* No explanation. Explanations get forwarded to friends and coach the next applicant.

Accepted responses route to a scheduling link. Confirmation email contains the §1.2 shelf note.

## 3. Stage B — the intake call: minute budget

Twenty minutes is not a lot. Print this table and keep it in view.

| Clock | Block | § |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:30 | Open, consent-on-record, frame | §3.1 |
| 1:30–3:00 | Confirmations (fast, from the Form) | §3.2 |
| 3:00–9:00 | **Reading history — four passes** | §4 |
| 9:00–11:00 | **Forward-looking triad — the incumbent is captured here** | §5 |
| 11:00–14:00 | **Recognition grid** | §6 |
| 14:00–16:30 | **Rating pass** | §7 |
| 16:30–18:00 | **The friend** | §8 |
| 18:00–20:00 | Logistics, what to bring, close | §9 |

**The order is not negotiable.** The incumbent book (§5) must be captured *after* the history passes — so the participant is in reading-brain and gives a real answer rather than a polite one — and *before* the recognition grid (§6), because sixty book covers will change what a person thinks they are reading next. If you run the grid first you have destroyed the instrument.

### 3.1 Opening — verbatim

> "Thanks for doing this. Before we start: I'm going to hit record now, and everything I said on the form still holds — it's private, it's not published, it's deleted at the end. Say 'yes' for the recording if you're still happy with that."
>
> *[wait for the yes]*
>
> "Great. So this is twenty minutes and it's almost all about what you've read. There are no right answers and I'm not testing your memory — I'll be surprised if you can remember more than about half of what you've actually read, nobody can. Just say whatever comes."

That last sentence matters. It pre-forgives the failure that is about to happen and it visibly lowers the stakes, which increases yield.

### 3.2 Confirmations — fast

Read back, don't re-ask:

> "You said about [N] books last year, you recommended something to someone in the last few months, and you use [tracker / nothing]. All still right?"
>
> "And you've got a shelf or a library app somewhere near you?"

If they do not: continue anyway, note `shelf_available = FALSE` in the sheet, and expect a title count 30–40% lower. This is a data-quality flag that must travel with the participant's row.

## 4. Reading history — the elicitation method

### 4.1 The method, named

**Four-pass prompted recall, with a recognition grid as a separate, separately-labelled backstop.**

Free recall is bad. A reader who has finished 22 books in a year will free-recall eight to twelve of them in six minutes, and the ones they recall are not a random eight — they are the most recent and the most extreme. Prompted recall organised by *retrieval context* (time, physical location, device, emotional salience) beats free recall substantially because it gives the memory a handle. Reading titles off an actual shelf beats everything, because it is not recall at all.

**Target: 22–28 titles recorded. Hard floor: 18.** Below 18 rated titles the overlap computation is too thin to distinguish an engineered-density reader from R4, which is the entire top row of the decision tree. A participant who lands below 18 gets one emailed follow-up ("could you send me ten more titles off your shelf, any ten?") and, if that fails, is replaced.

### 4.2 The four passes — verbatim

**Pass 1 — Recent (target 6–9 titles). 2 minutes.**

> "Start with this year. What have you finished in the last twelve months? Just call them out, in any order, and don't worry about getting it complete."

Then, when they run out — and they will run out fast, around the fifth title:

> "What about ones you didn't love? Those are harder to remember. Anything you finished and thought was fine, or a bit disappointing?"

*(This second prompt is doing real work. Positive-valence recall runs first and people stop when it dries up. Explicitly asking for the mediocre ones typically adds two to four titles, and those titles are disproportionately useful because overlap on a book both parties shrugged at is much more informative than overlap on a universally loved one.)*

**Pass 2 — Now and just-abandoned (target 1–3). 30 seconds.**

> "What are you in the middle of right now?"
> "Anything you started this year and put down?"

Mark abandoned titles `read_status = abandoned`. They are ratable and they count toward overlap.

**Pass 3 — Durable (target 4–6). 1.5 minutes.**

> "Now forget the last year. If someone asked you what you love — the books you'd actually name — what are they?"

Then the sharper version, which produces better titles than the general one:

> "Different question: what's the book you've pressed on the most people?"
> "And is there one you'd defend even though you know most people don't like it?"

Mark these `source = recall_identity`. They are the highest-signal titles in the whole set and they should be weighted accordingly when selecting which real list becomes R1–R3 for this participant.

**Pass 4 — The shelf sweep (target 8–14). 2 minutes. The highest-yield pass.**

> "Okay, last one and it's the easy one. Go to your shelf — or open [their tracker] — and just read me titles. Don't filter, don't explain, don't skip the embarrassing ones. Only rule is: only ones you've actually finished or actually abandoned. If it's unread, skip it."

Then, while they read:

> "Keep going."
> "That's great, keep going."

Say nothing else. Do not react to individual titles. Do not say "oh I love that one." The moderator's enthusiasm is a rating signal and it will contaminate §7.

Stop them at 2 minutes with:

> "Perfect, that's plenty."

Mark these `source = shelf_sweep`.

### 4.3 Stall prompts — verbatim, for "I can't remember what I've read"

Use in this order. Each one is a different retrieval handle.

1. > "Totally normal — nobody can do this from cold. Try it this way: where were you the last time you finished a book? On a train, in bed, on holiday? What was it?"
2. > "What did you read on your last holiday or over Christmas?"
3. > "Has anyone given you a book in the last year or two? What was it?"
4. > "What's the last book you talked to someone about — even if you didn't finish it, even if you were complaining about it?"
5. > "What's on your bedside table right now, or on the floor next to the bed?"
6. > "Open your Kindle, or your library app, or your Amazon orders, and just read me the last screen."
7. > "Think about a specific person you talk to about books. What did the two of you both read?"
8. > "Doesn't have to be recent. What did you read in your twenties that you'd still stand behind?"

If a participant is visibly embarrassed:

> "Genuinely — this is a memory task and memory is bad at this. The people who name forty titles are usually the ones reading off a screen. You're doing fine."

### 4.4 Be honest about what this method biases

Record this in the write-up as a stated limitation, not a footnote:

- **Recency bias.** The four-pass method over-samples the last twelve months. Books read five years ago are systematically under-represented unless they survive into Pass 3 as identity books.
- **Ownership bias.** The shelf sweep captures books the participant physically owns or bought digitally, and misses library borrowings, borrowed-from-friends, and anything read and given away. For a heavy library user this is a large hole.
- **Valence bias.** Even with the explicit mediocre-books prompt, the recalled set skews toward 4s and 5s. Expect the rating distribution to be top-heavy and do not treat that as a finding about the participant.
- **The grid (§6) is worse than all of these and is handled separately.**

Net effect: recorded histories are a **non-random subsample of roughly half** of what the participant has actually read, biased toward recent, owned, and liked. Every overlap number computed from them is therefore a **floor, not an estimate**. Say so out loud in the readout.

## 5. The forward-looking triad — where the incumbent is captured

This is the most important ninety seconds of the call and the participant must not know it.

**Ask all three. Ask them in this order. Ask them in the same tone.** The middle one is the instrument; the other two are camouflage and are genuinely recorded (they are useful colour, and they make the middle question unremarkable).

> **Q-A.** "What's physically waiting for you — anything on the bedside table or in a pile that you own and haven't read?"

> **Q-B.** "And when you finish [their current book], what's the one you'll actually pick up next? Not what you *should* read — the one you'll actually reach for."

> **Q-C.** "Anything you've been meaning to get to for years and honestly probably never will?"

### 5.1 Nailing down Q-B — the incumbent

Q-B must resolve to **exactly one title.** If they name two or more:

> "If you had to pick one to be next — which one?"

If they genuinely cannot pick:

> "Okay — say you finish tonight and you have to start something tomorrow morning. Which one is it?"

If they still cannot: record `incumbent = NONE`. Do **not** invent one and do not let them pick one at the session. A participant with no incumbent is run as a **separate, labelled sub-group** and reported separately — their "displacement" is free and means nothing. Expect one to three of these. They are not failures; they are a real state, and they are informative about whether the next-book question is even live for this reader.

Then, casually, ask all three of these about **all three** answers so the incumbent is not singled out:

> "And are those pretty settled, or could they change?"
> `Locked` / `Probably` / `Could easily change`

> "Do you own the copies, or would you be buying or borrowing?"
> `Own physical` / `Own digital` / `Library hold` / `Would have to get it`

And the one that matters most:

> "For that middle one — say in one sentence why that's the one."

**Capture the answer word for word.** This sentence goes on the incumbent card in §11. If they give three sentences, ask:

> "If you had to put that on the back of the book, what's the line?"

### 5.2 What gets recorded

`incumbent_title`, `incumbent_author`, `incumbent_firmness` (Locked / Probably / Could easily change), `incumbent_possession` (Own physical / Own digital / Library hold / Would have to get it), `incumbent_reason_verbatim`, `incumbent_captured_ts`.

Read the title and author back before moving on:

> "Just so I've got it right — [Title], by [Author]. Yeah?"

Getting the title wrong on the card at the session is a fatal, unrecoverable error. Confirm it.

### 5.3 What must never be said

Never say, at intake or before the moment of choice: *reading next*, *commit*, *slot*, *choose*, *swap*, *instead of*, *give up*, or anything implying the next book will matter later. If a participant asks why you want to know, the true and sufficient answer is:

> "I'm interested in how people decide, so I ask everyone what's next."

## 6. The recognition grid — the overlap backstop

**3 minutes. Runs after §5, never before.**

### 6.1 What it is

Four screen-shared slides, fifteen covers each, sixty covers total. Composition:

- **~30 titles drawn from the union of the reader-list bank** (the 12–20 real published lists from which R1–R4 are selected per participant).
- **~30 decoy titles** matched on publication decade and popularity tier, drawn from *no* list in the bank.

Decoys are not optional. Without them the grid is a list of the correct answers and the participant will later notice that every book on Reader 2's shelf was on your slide.

### 6.2 Script — verbatim

> "Last memory thing and it's the easy kind. I'm going to share four screens of covers. Just tell me if you've read any of them. Don't explain, don't justify, and 'started and gave up' counts — just say 'gave up'."

Advance every 40 seconds:

> "Anything on this one?"
> "Next."

Do not comment on any title. Do not linger. If they ask what the grid is:

> "It's a fixed set I show everyone — it's just faster than asking you to remember."

True.

### 6.3 The bias this introduces, and exactly how it is handled

Recognition prompting inflates recall. That is the point. But it inflates it **selectively toward the titles on the reader lists**, which is the exact quantity under test. If you compute overlap from grid-derived titles and report it as the participant's overlap with Reader 2, you have manufactured the finding.

**The rule, and it is absolute:**

- Every title carries `source` ∈ {`recall_recent`, `recall_identity`, `shelf_sweep`, `grid`}.
- **Overlap for selection** — deciding which real list becomes R1, R2, R3 for this participant — uses **all** titles including grid titles. Selection is allowed to be well-informed; selection is not a finding.
- **Overlap reported as the density figure on any card, in any table, in the readout** uses **recall + shelf sweep only**, excluding grid titles. This is the honest number and it is the one that goes on the reader profile sheet.
- **R4's thinness is computed recall-only.** If R4 shows 3–4 shared books recall-only, that is the finding. Do not "improve" it with grid titles.
- Report both numbers side by side per participant. If grid-inclusive overlap is dramatically higher than recall-only overlap across the sample, that gap is itself a reportable fact about how much of a reader's history is invisible to any product that relies on what they can remember to log.

## 7. The rating pass

**2.5 minutes. Rapid. Moderator reads, participant answers.**

### 7.1 The scale, and why this one

**Five points, verbally anchored, with an explicit escape hatch, plus one binary.**

| | Anchor — read aloud once at the start, then never again |
|---|---|
| **1** | "Didn't like it. Wouldn't have finished it if I weren't the kind of person who finishes things." |
| **2** | "Fine. Nothing." |
| **3** | "Good. Glad I read it." |
| **4** | "Very good. It stayed with me." |
| **5** | "One of the ones I'd name if someone asked what I love." |
| **X** | "Read it, genuinely can't rate it — too long ago, remember nothing." |

Then, only on 4s and 5s:

> **"Press?"** — "Would you put this in a specific person's hands?" `Y` / `N`

**Justification for five points.** Three reasons, in order of weight.

1. **It is the reader-native scale.** Goodreads and StoryGraph have trained this population on five stars for fifteen years. A 7- or 10-point scale requires the participant to learn a mapping mid-call, which costs time we do not have and produces anchoring noise in the first six items.
2. **Speed.** Twenty-five titles at six seconds each is 2.5 minutes. A 10-point scale roughly doubles per-item latency because it forces a genuine comparison rather than a category assignment. In a 20-minute call the scale must be cheap.
3. **The reader side constrains us anyway.** The four readers are real people with real published lists, and published lists mostly do not carry numeric ratings — a critic's year-end list is a binary endorsement, a signed bookseller pick is a binary endorsement. The P condition therefore displays the reader's judgment **in the source's own words** ("one of her ten best books of 2025"; "signed staff pick, [store]"; "her number one of the year", where the source actually ranks). We never fabricate a number for a real person. Given that the reader side is effectively ordinal-and-coarse, extra granularity on the participant side buys nothing for overlap computation.

**The known weakness, stated plainly:** five points compresses at the top. Because the elicitation is already valence-biased (§4.4), expect a distribution heavy in 4s and 5s and thin in 1s and 2s. This is why the **Press** binary exists — it is the discriminator the stars cannot provide, splitting "very good" from "part of who I am" without adding a scale point. Use Press, not the star, whenever a tie-break is needed in reader selection or card construction.

**The X option is mandatory.** Without it, participants fabricate ratings for half-remembered books to avoid seeming ignorant, and fabricated ratings are the worst possible input to an agreement computation. Say it out loud:

> "'X' is a completely normal answer, use it freely."

### 7.2 Script

> "Right — I'm going to read your list back and you give me a number one to five, or X. Here's what the numbers mean: [read anchors once]. Fast as you can, first instinct."

Then, one title per breath: *"[Title]." — "4." — "[Title]." — "X." — "[Title]." — "5."*

On a 4 or 5, immediately: *"Press?"* — *"Yeah."*

If they start explaining a rating:

> "Save it — I'd love to hear that but the clock's against us. Just the number."

If they ask what a book "should" be:

> "There's no should. Yours."

## 8. The friend (for S2)

**90 seconds.**

> "Different sort of question. Is there one person in your life you'd actually send a book to — not tag in something, not mention it to, but properly say 'read this'?"

> "Who are they to you?" `[relationship]`

> "First name is fine — what do I call them?" `[first name only]`

> "And how would that actually happen? Text, in person, you'd buy it and hand it over?"

> "Last thing: what was the last book that went between you, in either direction? And roughly when?"

**Stall prompt** if they say there isn't one:

> "Doesn't have to be a close friend — could be a colleague, a sibling, someone you barely see. Just someone where 'you should read this' from you would actually land."

If there is genuinely no such person, record `friend = NONE`. That is a real and important data point for a product built on the two-person loop and it must not be smoothed over.

**Privacy rule, absolute:** collect **first name, relationship, channel, and last exchange only.** Never collect the friend's surname, phone number, email, or social handle. Nothing in either experiment contacts this person. The friend exists in the data as an anchor for the participant's own reasoning, not as a lead.

## 9. Close — verbatim

> "That's it, you're done with the hard part. The session's [date/time], it's forty-five minutes, and there's genuinely nothing to prepare — please don't read up on anything or look anything up, it'll actually make it worse. Just turn up.
>
> One thing: don't go and read anything unusual between now and then just because we talked about books. Carry on exactly as you were.
>
> And I'll send you two short messages after — one about a week later, one about three weeks later. One line each, thirty seconds of your life."

The "carry on exactly as you were" line is doing real work: it is a weak but non-zero guard against the participant reshuffling their reading in the seven days between intake and session, which would silently invalidate the incumbent.

**Do not tell them to bring their next book. Do not mention the next book again.**

## PART TWO — THE COMMITMENT-DISPLACEMENT INSTRUMENT

*Implements memo §8.1 exactly: one visible slot, pre-filled at intake with the participant's own stated next book, displaced only by a Dewey card, keeping presented as normal and expected, no purchase, no mailing, no reward.*

## 11. Physical form

### 11.1 The board

One piece of stiff board, roughly A4, matte, no branding, no logo, no colour. Cut or mounted so it holds **exactly one card** — a single pocket or a pair of corner slots. Above the pocket, in small plain type, one word:

> **READING NEXT**

That is the entire apparatus. Nothing else on the board. No second slot, no "maybe" tray, no ranking area. The affordance must be unambiguous: **one thing fits here.**

### 11.2 The cards — all four identical in every respect except content

A6 (105 × 148 mm), 300gsm, same stock, same typeface, same layout skeleton, same margins, same cover-image size, same vertical extent. Four cards on the table each session: the incumbent card, plus P, N, and G.

**The incumbent card carries:**

- The cover image, same size as on every other card
- Title and author, same type, same position
- One line of evidence, in the same slot where P/N/G carry theirs:

> *"You told me on [date]: '[their verbatim sentence from §5.1]'."*

**This is a deliberate handicap on the treatment, and it is the correct choice.** If the incumbent card were a bare cover while the P card carried a face, a rating, and three named books, the study would be measuring visual richness, not attribution. Giving the incumbent the participant's own words in their own voice makes it the *most* personally weighted card on the table. If a Dewey card still displaces it, the displacement is worth something. If we had let the null action be visually impoverished, no result would have meant anything.

Where the incumbent is `NONE`: the slot holds a blank card reading only **"Nothing decided yet."** These participants are analysed separately and their displacement is never pooled with the rest.

### 11.3 Placement and timing

The board sits on the table, incumbent card already in the slot, **before the participant enters the room or joins the call.** They see it from minute zero. It is furniture, not a prop that appears at the dramatic moment.

At the very start of the session, once, plainly, then never referred to again until §12:

> "That's your next book, from our call. It's yours. It stays there unless you move it."

Then move on to the reader profiles. Do not touch the board again for thirty minutes.

## 12. The moment of choice — moderator script, verbatim

The three condition cards are placed face-up in a row **below** the board, in the order given by `card_order_seed`, all three at once. Never sequentially — sequential presentation creates a recency effect that would swamp the manipulation.

> **"Okay. Here are three books. All three are books you told me you haven't read — is that right for all three?"**
>
> *[Verification. If any is read: remove it, insert the pre-prepared backup card in the same condition, note the swap in `notes`. Two backups per condition are prepared for every session.]*
>
> **"Take as long as you want with them. Read everything that's on them. Pick them up, move them around, put them in whatever order you like."**

**Then stop talking.** Minimum thirty seconds of silence. Do not fill it. Do not explain. Start the stopwatch for `seconds_to_choice`.

When they look up, or at ninety seconds, whichever is first:

> **"Now — the card in the slot is [Incumbent Title], which you told me on our call is the one you're reading next. There's one slot. If you want one of these three in it, you take yours out and put theirs in. If you want to keep yours, you keep yours — plenty of people do, and it isn't the wrong answer. Whatever's in the slot when we finish is what you're telling me you're actually reading next."**
>
> **"Go ahead. Then tell me why."**

**Then stop talking again.** Say nothing at all until the card is placed. Not "take your time," not "no rush," not a nod. Every sound the moderator makes at this moment is a thumb on the scale.

### 12.1 If they hesitate or ask a question

Only these responses. Nothing else.

- *"Can I pick two?"* → **"One slot, sorry."**
- *"What happens if I choose one?"* → **"Nothing happens. I write down what's in the slot."**
- *"Do I have to?"* → **"No. Keeping yours is completely fine."**
- *"Which one do most people pick?"* → **"I'm not going to tell you that — it'd wreck the answer. Whichever you actually want."**
- *"Is this a trick?"* → **"No trick. There's no right card."**
- Silence past two minutes → **"Whenever you're ready. And keeping yours is a real option, not a cop-out."**

### 12.2 Making "keep my own book" genuinely normal

Three mechanisms, all of which are already in the script above, listed here so they are not edited out by someone tidying the wording:

1. **The incumbent starts in the slot.** Keeping requires no action. In every choice architecture the default is the low-effort option, and here the default is deliberately assigned to the null. We are stacking the room against our own hypothesis on purpose.
2. **"Plenty of people do, and it isn't the wrong answer."** Stated *before* the choice, not after. Said after, it is consolation; said before, it is permission.
3. **The incumbent card is the richest card on the table** (§11.2), carrying the participant's own words.

The one thing that would destroy all three: a moderator who leans forward when the participant reaches for a Dewey card and sits back when they don't. Record the session. Watch your own first three sessions back before running the fourth.

## 13. Immediately after the choice — the part that is the finding

Regardless of which card is in the slot:

> **"Say it in one sentence. Why that one."**

Write it down verbatim. This is `sentence_verbatim` and it is the primary output of the instrument.

Then **exactly one** probe, matched to the outcome:

- **If a Dewey card was placed:** > **"What made you sure?"**
- **If they kept their own:** > **"What would one of those have had to be, for you to move yours?"**

One probe. Not two. A second probe produces post-hoc rationalisation, which reads like insight and is worthless.

Then, only if the participant chose **P**, and phrased so it does not lead:

> **"Describe [Reader name] to me. Not whether you like them — what are they like as a reader?"**

Record as `reader_description_verbatim`. This is the stop-condition measure. (The same probe is asked about the chosen reader in the profile portion of the session for every participant, regardless of card choice; it is repeated here only when P was chosen, so the description is attached to the moment of choice.)

### 13.1 Coding the description — the falsifiability test

A description counts as **specific and falsifiable** only if it names something that could be checked against a book and could turn out to be wrong.

**Counts:**
- "She likes books where basically nothing happens and the sentences are doing all the work."
- "He's the kind of person who reads translated fiction because it's translated."
- "She'll forgive a bad plot for a good voice but not the other way round."
- "This is someone who reads the second-best book by famous authors."

**Does not count:**
- "She has great taste." / "Really interesting person." / "Seems well read." / "Similar to me." / "Reads a lot of literary fiction." *(too broad to be wrong)*

Two independent coders, blind to card choice. Disagreements resolved by a third read of the transcript, not by discussion. Record `falsifiable_coder1` and `falsifiable_coder2` and report raw agreement as a count.

**Stop condition:** ≤ 8 of 20 producing a specific, falsifiable description kills the thesis. This is the one threshold in this document that licenses a decision, and it licenses a **stop**, never a build.

## 14. What is said afterward — regardless of choice, verbatim

Say this to every participant, in the same tone, whether they displaced or kept:

> **"Thank you. Quick thing so you're not wondering: there was no right answer there, and I'd have asked you exactly the same questions whichever card ended up in the slot. People go both ways on it and both are useful to me. I'm not testing you — I'm testing the cards."**

**Never quote a running tally.** Do not say "about half of people keep theirs" or "you're the third person to pick that one." At participant 6 you would be quoting a number of five, and by participant 15 the sentence has become a social norm you injected into your own study. If a participant asks how others chose:

> **"I'll tell you at the end of the whole thing if you want — I can't while it's running."**

Then close:

> **"Last thing: I'll message you in about a week and ask what you're reading. There's no right answer to that either, and it genuinely doesn't matter to me which book it is — I just want to know what actually happened. If you read neither of these and picked up something completely different, that's the answer I want."**

That last sentence is load-bearing. It pre-releases the participant from the obligation to perform follow-through, which is the single largest threat to the day-7 measure.

## 15. The follow-up measure

### 15.1 Day 7 — the message

Sent 7 days after the session, ±1 day, on the channel recorded at `followup_channel`, at roughly the same time of day the session ran.

> **"Hi [first name] — one quick question, then I'll leave you alone. What are you reading right now, or what's the next one you'll pick up? One line is fine, and there's no right answer."**

**It does not name any book.** Not the incumbent, not the chosen card. Naming either one cues the answer and turns a behaviour measure into a recall test.

### 15.2 The verification step

If they name a book, send **one** message and only one:

> **"Nice — where are you in it?"**

That is the verification. It is deliberately trivial to answer honestly and awkward to answer falsely.

### 15.3 What counts as evidence

| Tier | Definition | Counted as |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Verified start** | Names the book **and** supplies one unpromptable detail: a position ("about sixty pages in", "just finished part one"), a reaction to the opening, a photo of the copy, or the format they got it in. | **A start.** The only thing that is. |
| **2 — Acquired, not started** | Bought it, borrowed it, has a library hold, downloaded a sample. Evidenced. Not reading it. | Recorded. **Never a start.** |
| **3 — Stated intent** | "Yeah, that one's next." "I'm going to get to it." No book in hand, no position. | Recorded as intent. **Counted as nothing.** |
| **0 — No reply** after one reminder at day 9, closed at day 10 | | **No data.** Reported in the denominator, never imputed as a null. |

Tiers 2 and 3 are the follow-up-stage expression of the memo's rule that **saves are a lying metric.** A participant who bought the book and never opened it has produced a save. Reporting that as a success would be exactly the intent theatre the memo's decision tree row 7 is built to catch.

### 15.4 Day 21

One message, free recall, no cue:

> **"Last one, promise — what have you read since we talked?"**

Compare against the incumbent, the three cards, and everything else. Record `day21_titles`.

Day 21 is **descriptive only.** It is not a measure and no decision hangs on it. Its function is to catch the case where a displacement at day 7 evaporated by day 21, which is worth knowing and is not worth pretending we can quantify at N=20.

### 15.5 The limitation that must be stated with the day-7 result

The participant knows the researcher asked. A person who wants to be a good participant can buy and start the book on day 6 specifically because a message is coming. The verification detail resists this **weakly** — someone motivated to please can read forty pages. Demand characteristics inflate day-7 starts in every arm, including the null action, and the only defence is the ratio between arms, not the absolute number. Report the absolute count as a curiosity and the between-arm comparison as the finding, and label both.

## 16. Debrief — at the end of the session, after §14

Read this. Do not paraphrase it.

> **"Now I'll tell you what this actually was. Those four readers are real people. Those are their real, published book lists — a year-end list, a bookshop's signed staff picks, a newspaper interview. I've written the sources on this sheet with links, so you can go and look. They have nothing to do with me and nothing to do with this study; they don't know it exists and they haven't endorsed anything. I used public lists with attribution because inventing four fake readers would have meant testing whether my made-up people are legible, which isn't a useful thing to know.**
>
> **The three cards said different true things about the same kinds of book — one had a person on it, one had the same evidence with no person, one had neither. That was the thing I was testing, and that's why I couldn't tell you up front.**
>
> **Did you recognise any of those four, or any of the lists?"**

Hand over the printed source sheet: four readers, four sources, four links.

**If they recognised a source or the person:** record `recognition_flag_list_id` and **exclude that reader's data for that participant**, per memo §7.3. Their other readers stay in.

Then:

> **"Anything you'd want me to take out of the recording?"**

Honour it.

## 17. What this instrument does and does not prove

### It establishes

**That a real cost was paid, or wasn't.** A participant who moves a Dewey card into the slot has withdrawn a book they named unprompted, in their own words, seven days earlier — a book many of them already own. That is a revealed preference under an opportunity cost, not a rating on a survey. It is a small cost. It is not zero, and it is more than any stated-preference instrument produces.

**A within-participant comparison where only one thing varied.** All three condition cards are drawn from the same pool, matched on popularity tier, visual weight, vertical extent and typography, and verified unread. Every statement on all three is true of the book shown. What varies is **which true fact is surfaced.** That is a clean manipulation, and the presence of N is what makes it worth running at all — without N, a P-over-G result is fully explained by "the card named three books I love," which requires no person and no graph.

**A moment where somebody has to say why.** This is the actual product of the instrument. Twenty people, forced off the fence, articulating a reason in one sentence while the reason is still hot. **The sentence is the finding.** Everything else here is scaffolding to produce twenty sentences that could not have been produced by asking people what they think of an idea.

### It does not establish

- **That they will read it.** A card in a slot is not a book in a hand. The day-7 measure gestures at this and does so weakly.
- **That the preference survives a week.** Or a day. Or the walk to the car.
- **That it survives outside a room with a researcher in it.** Moderator presence, a printed board, and the felt obligation of a scheduled session are all pushing in the same direction, and none of them ship.
- **That it survives at real network density.** The overlap on R1–R3 was selected. Real networks do not select. R4 is the only card here that resembles reality, and its whole purpose is to tell us whether the rest of the study is describing a world that exists.
- **Trust.** Nothing here measures trust. Choosing a card because a stranger's shelf looked interesting is curiosity, and curiosity is cheap and decays.
- **Anything about the app.** There is no app. Whether Dewey feels good is a different question and this study is silent on it.
- **Anything at all when expressed as a percentage.** N=20. **A 14/20 split is comfortably inside the range that twenty coin flips produce.** Report "14 of 20." Never "70%," never "68.3%," never a p-value, never a confidence interval, never a bar chart with a y-axis in percent. This study cannot produce statistical significance and is not trying to. Anyone who converts these counts to a rate has made the number look like evidence it is not.

### The warning

> **No threshold on this measure licenses a build decision.** There is no split — not 15/20, not 18/20 — that means "build it." If you find yourself computing a conversion rate from this instrument, you have misused it, and the misuse will feel like rigour at exactly the moment it stops being rigour.

The instrument has one job: force a real choice so that a real sentence comes out. Read the twenty sentences. If they are specific, falsifiable, and about a person, the thesis has something under it and the field study (Experiment 2) is the thing that tests it with verified starts. If they are vague, or about the book jacket, or about nothing, then no split in the counts rescues it.

The only threshold in this entire document that licenses a decision is the stop condition, and it licenses a stop: **≤ 8 of 20 producing a specific, falsifiable description of the reader they chose** means a shelf of covers and numbers does not resolve into a person, provenance has nothing to attach to, and Dewey must be expression-driven or must not be built.
